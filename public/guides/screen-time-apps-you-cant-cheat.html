Screen Time Apps Compared: Which Ones Can You Actually Cheat?

Feature lists are not the useful comparison. There is one question that predicts whether a blocker will still be running next month.

8 min read · Updated August 2026

Screen time apps are usually compared on features: schedules, statistics, categories, widgets. That comparison is close to useless, because almost all of them do those things and none of it predicts whether the app is still running in a month.

The question that predicts it is narrower: at the moment you most want in, can you undo the block by yourself?

Four models, and what each survives

Friction — one sec, ScreenZen

A pause, a breath, an interruption screen before the app opens. You can always continue.

Survives: unconscious reaching, which is a large share of all phone pickups. Genuinely effective, and the least unpleasant thing on this list.

Does not survive: a decision you actually made. That is by design, not a defect — these apps are built on the belief that you should stay in control, and for many people that is the correct trade.

Timers — Forest, most focus apps

You start a session, and a countdown holds until it ends.

Survives: distraction inside a work block you chose to begin.

Does not survive: evenings and weekends, because you simply never start a session. An opt-in tool is only as reliable as the moment you opt in — and the moment you most need it is exactly the moment you will not.

Self-held passcode — Apple Screen Time, most strict modes

A real block, with a code you chose.

Survives: mild impulse. The prompt itself adds a useful beat.

Does not survive: genuine craving. You know the code. This is where the overwhelming majority of people fail, and it is a structural problem rather than a personal one.

Opal's Deep Focus belongs roughly here too — deliberately hard to reverse, and its own documentation suggests handing your phone to someone else to set a passcode you do not know. That works, and it is telling: the strongest version of a self-held lock is the one where it stops being self-held.

Another person holds it — Disconnect and a handful of others

The decision genuinely leaves your hands. Two versions exist.

The improvised one is asking a friend to type a passcode you never see. Free, works immediately, and collapses within a fortnight — not because the idea is wrong but because they have to be physically present every time you have a legitimate reason to get in.

The built-in one is a request-and-approve flow: you ask remotely, they answer in one tap. Same principle, without the logistics. Disconnect, LockPact, PeerPause and a few others work this way.

The comparison that matters

Laid against the single question:

  • one sec, ScreenZen — yes, you can continue. Deliberately.
  • Forest — yes, you can end the session.
  • Apple Screen Time — yes, you hold the passcode.
  • Opal — mostly no in Deep Focus, and stronger still if someone else sets your code.
  • Disconnect (Hard lock) — no. Only your buddy can approve, and approvals expire on their own.

None of these is the best app. They are answers to different questions, and picking the wrong one is why people conclude that blockers do not work.

How to choose

Be honest about your actual failure mode, because they need different tools.

  • You open apps without noticing. Friction. ScreenZen is free and very good at this. You do not need anything stronger, and stronger will feel like a punishment.
  • You get pulled away during work. A timer, or a scheduled block during work hours.
  • You lose whole evenings on purpose and regret it after. Nothing you can undo alone will hold. This is the case that needs another person, and it is the only case where it is worth the small social cost of asking.

Most people reach for the strictest tool for a problem that only needed friction, find it oppressive, and abandon the category entirely. Match the tool to the failure.

Common questions

Quick answers.

Which screen time app can't be bypassed?

No app on your own phone is truly unbypassable while you control it, because you can always uninstall or reset. The closest are apps where another person approves each unlock, since no setting on your device lifts the block on its own.

Is Opal or Disconnect stricter?

They differ in kind rather than degree. Opal's Deep Focus is a strong self-held lock that resists changes; Disconnect's Hard lock moves the decision to another person entirely. Opal's own guidance suggests having someone else set your passcode, which is the same principle by a manual route.

Are free screen time apps good enough?

Often yes. ScreenZen is fully free with no paid tier, and Disconnect includes all three lock levels, one rule and one buddy for free. Paid tiers usually buy more rules and more flexibility, not stronger blocking.

Why do screen time apps stop working after a few weeks?

Usually because the off switch was in your hands the whole time. People rarely quit deliberately — they unlock once for a legitimate reason, never re-enable the rule, and drift back over the following weeks.
